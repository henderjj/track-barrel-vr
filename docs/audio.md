# Tank audio and the intercom (F1.12, sound pass F6.1)

The tank has a voice: an engine that follows the revs and the throttle, gears whining, tracks rumbling, clattering and squealing, the power traverse, the handwheels' gears, and short sounds for the gearbox, the hull bumping, the loading and the gun. The crewmate's voice plays plain, like a tank intercom in the crew's headphones, and the tank's sounds drop 12 dB while either player speaks so the intercom stays clear (concept §6, plan §4.8).

Everything is synthesised with Web Audio at load: no sample files, nothing from a CDN.

F1.12's sounds were placeholders (John's play test, 2026-11-07). The sound pass (F6.1) rebuilt every one of them on the real tank: what each thing is made of, how often it is struck, and what the crew would hear of it inside a steel box. John chose modelled sounds over recordings (2026-11-09): they follow the revs, the speed and the controls exactly and need no licences or downloads, and any one of them can still be swapped for a recording later.

John then sent a recording of a real Sherman driving past (2026-11-12), and the engine, the tracks and the way sounds are placed were tuned against it: see [Measured against a real Sherman](#measured-against-a-real-sherman). He also asked whether the sounds should vary with the ground: they do, each track for itself: see [What the tracks run on](#what-the-tracks-run-on).

## What players hear

- **The engine**, all the time. An M4A4's engine is Chrysler's A57 multibank: five six-cylinder petrol car engines geared round one shaft, thirty cylinders. It is not a diesel, and it doesn't knock: at idle it is a deep burble, five sixes not quite in step, and as it revs the burble quickens into a rough, dense drone, not a note. Each bank fires three times a turn, so the banks' beat is 40 a second at idle and 130 flat out, but no two cylinders are alike, so what is heard is the whole uneven pattern coming round every two turns. It follows the RPM gauge exactly, so a gear change sounds like one. Pulling, it is louder and harder-edged, and a roar of fan, intake and burning rises over the exhaust, fluttering as the strong cylinders blow; with the throttle shut at the same revs it falls back to a hum. It hunts a little at idle, as engines do.
- **The gears**. A Sherman's gearbox and final drives are in the nose beside the driver, and their straight-cut gears whine: the gearbox with the engine (quiet out of gear), the final drives with the tracks, a low growl that climbs to about 250 Hz at top speed.
- **The tracks**, while the tank moves (a pivot turn too). A rumble through the floor that opens up with speed; each track's links meeting its sprocket, one clank per 15 cm of track, with the lesser knocks of the same links meeting the wheels and the idler between them, so at walking pace you hear each link in a rattle and at speed they run together into a clatter with the links' beat in it; and now and then a dry pin squealing, most in a turn. The left track is heard on the left and the right on the right, and in a turn the inner one clatters slower than the outer. Each track sounds of what it is on: duller and softer on grass, as tuned on the dirt roads, and brighter with the crunch of stones under it on the course's gravel ([What the tracks run on](#what-the-tracks-run-on)).
- **The power traverse**: the electric motor's whine, the gear train growling under it and the turret ring rumbling round, all rising with the turret's speed.
- **The handwheels**: the traverse crank's and the elevation wheel's gears tick as the gunner turns them, 14 ticks a turn.
- **The gearbox**: the lever clicking through its gate, then the gear going home with a thud in the cast case; and for as long as the gears grind (shifting with a tiller pushed, docs/driver-station.md), teeth clashing 60 times a second with a rasp between.
- **The engine starting and stopping** (F1.17, [engine.md](engine.md)): the starter's whine sagging as each bank comes up on compression, eight times a second; a ragged run of firings quickening into a bark as it catches; a hollow pop when a cold engine misfires; and a stall's few weakening beats before the engine shudders to rest. Run down or run up, the engine's own burble slows or quickens with the revs. The siren, held on the driver's switch strip, wails at 620 Hz on both pages.
- **A bump**: a deep thud through the floor, the armour groaning and the stowage rattling.
- **The loading**: the shell sliding up the chamber and its rim stopping against the breech face; the block sliding across and slamming shut on a heavy lump of steel; and, opened by hand, the catch clicking and the block stopping against its buffer.
- **The gun**, from inside. The blast slams through the armour as a crack with no top to it and a boom you feel; the hull rings like a struck tank; the gun slams back, the breech flies open and throws the case out; the gun runs out and thuds into battery; everything loose in the turret jumps; and the report rolls back off the range, twice, then a third time further off. For a moment after it the crew hear little else: the engine and tracks dip to a third and come back over a second. Half a second after the shot the brass case lands in the bin, clangs, bounces lower and sooner each time, and rolls to rest.
- **The cabin**. Every sound also goes through the echo of a small steel box: reflections off walls a metre or two away and a short tail that loses its top quickly. It is what makes the breech sound as if it is in the tank with you.
- **Where sounds come from**. Each is heard from its place in the tank as you turn your head: the engine behind the bulkhead, the gearbox ahead and to the driver's right, a track at each side, the breech and the casing bin with the turret wherever it points, the traverse gear by the gunner's right hand, the siren out on the left front mudguard. The rest of each sound reaches you from all round through the cabin's echo, as it does in a steel hull: the place is a lean, not a pinpoint.
- **The intercom**: the crewmate's voice, plain (not positioned). While either player's voice is above the speaking level, the tank's sounds dip by 12 dB within a twentieth of a second, hold through the gaps between words, and come back over about half a second.

## How the sounds are made

Three files: the rules in `src/sim/tank-audio.ts`, the samples in `src/sim/sound-render.ts` (both engine-free and unit-tested), and the Web Audio graph in `src/systems/tank-sound-graph.ts`, which `TankAudioSystem` drives. The graph knows nothing of the game, so `scripts/render-sound-preview.mjs` can run the same graph offline.

- **Something strikes, something rings.** A short sound is a blow (a low thump, a burst of noise, steel sliding on steel) and the ring of what was struck: a set of modes, each a decaying sine. The bodies are armour plate (26 modes from 105 Hz, the lowest ringing half a second), the breech's steel, a brass case, the bin's sheet metal, the gearbox's cast case, gear teeth and loose kit. Plate and cast steel ring at frequencies that are no harmonics of each other, which is why they clang and don't sing.
- **What moves, and what stays put.** The first engine sounded like a toy because its whole sound slid up and down with the revs. In a real one only the source changes: the firings come faster, but the exhaust pipes, the silencers and the hull they are heard through ring where they always did. So the engine is one loop of exhaust pulses (every cylinder's, over 16 four-stroke cycles) played as fast as the engine turns, through filters that don't move with the revs; its roar is noise let through by a second loop in step with the first, a breath for every firing. The tracks work the same way: a gate opens for each strike of a link, faster as the track runs faster, and lets noise into resonances that stay where they are, so the links come quicker without turning into a higher note.
- **Nothing is quite even, but the unevenness is the engine's own.** Thirty perfectly even cylinders would make one hum at 200 Hz, and five even sixes one note at the banks' beat. The banks aren't timed against each other, no bank is as loud at the crew's ears as the next, and each cylinder has its own strength, its own sharpness and its own length of pipe, which brings its pulse early or late: that is the burble. The pattern is the same every cycle (the real recording shows it is: its tones stand clear of what lies between them), and only a little changes from one firing to the next. No two links land as hard, no two blows on the casing excite the same mix of its modes.
- **Once, not twice.** A placed sound is heard through the browser's head-related panning, which comes out about 6 ms late. Played straight as well (as the first sound pass did, for the share heard from all round), the two cancel each other every 160 Hz up the scale: a hollow, pipe-like tone on everything, sweeping as the head turns. So nothing placed is also played straight; the share from all round goes through the cabin's echo, which is many reflections and no one delay.
- **Low, but not too low.** A headset's speakers pass little under 100 Hz, so the rumble has nothing below 38 Hz to waste the headroom, and the engine, the tracks and the gun all carry most of what they have between 100 and 1000 Hz. Levels fall away steadily above that, as they do inside real tracked vehicles.

## What the tracks run on

The world has three surfaces, and the sound follows what is drawn: grass everywhere, the range's two dirt roads (`onRoad`, `src/sim/range.ts`) and the biathlon course's gravel track with its penalty loop (`onGravel`, `src/sim/course.ts`, to the gravel's own width, not the wider strip that scatter keeps off). The rules are in `src/sim/ground-surface.ts`.

- **Each track for itself.** A track lies on the ground from its first road wheel to its last, 3.96 m, 1.41 m out from the hull's middle. Its footing is judged at five points along that line: the share of them on each surface is the share of each surface's sound. So with the left track on the road and the right on the verge, the left is heard on the left as a track on dirt and the right on the right as a track on grass; and a track crossing a road's edge end on changes over a fifth at a time as it goes, not all at once (about a second at a walking pace, a third of a second at top speed).
- **What the ground changes.** The links come as often and as hard whatever is under them, so their rate and level don't change. What changes is what is heard of each strike, and what the ground adds:
  - the links' lower ring (the bands at 330, 590 and 1040 Hz): turf beds a link in and damps it;
  - their clink (the bands at 1730, 2600 and 4300 Hz): steel on something hard, the part that changes most;
  - each link's thud through the floor;
  - the ground's own noise under the links, which is new: grains of noise, one for each stone crushed or flung or each tuft torn, in a band that is the ground's own. On grass it is a low rustle, on dirt a little grit, on gravel a bright crunch as loud as the links' clink. At a crawl each grain is heard; at speed they run together;
  - the rumble through the floor, a little duller and quieter on grass and busier on gravel, and the pins' squeal, less on grass (a track slips on turf where it bites on a road). Both come from the two tracks together, so they take the mean of what the two are on.
- **Dirt is the sound as it was.** The tracks were tuned (against the real recording, above) with nothing known about the ground, so that sound is now the dirt road's, and every factor is 1 there. Grass is duller than before and gravel brighter.
- **A new surface is one row.** Cobbles, tarmac or a bridge's planks need a name in `SURFACES`, a row in `SURFACE_SOUND` and a rule in `surfaceAt` saying where it is. Nothing else knows the surfaces by name: the footing, the track's voice, the graph and the tour work from the rows. A hard surface would have a high clink and thud and a squeal above 1.

| Surface | Lower ring | Clink | Thud | Ground's noise | Its band | Rumble | Rumble's low-pass | Squeal |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Grass | 0.85 | 0.5 | 0.7 | 0.5 | 750 Hz | 0.9 | × 0.75 | 0.6 |
| Dirt road | 1 | 1 | 1 | 0.45 | 1600 Hz | 1 | × 1 | 1 |
| Gravel | 1.05 | 1.2 | 1.1 | 1 | 3000 Hz | 1.08 | × 1.2 | 0.85 |

Measured on the rendered clip (`ground-tracks`, below; tracks only, at 14 km/h): against the dirt road, grass is 1 to 2 dB quieter up to 1 kHz and 5 dB quieter from 1.6 kHz up; gravel is about 1 dB louder up to 1 kHz, 3 dB at 2 kHz and 6 to 8 dB from 3 kHz up. From grass to gravel is 11 to 13 dB above 3 kHz. With the engine and gears over them, most of the difference between grass and gravel is above 1.5 kHz: 2 dB at 2 kHz, 7 dB at 3 kHz and 10 to 12 dB above that. Below, the engine covers most of it: 1 to 2 dB where the rumble and the thud lie (under 400 Hz) and nothing from 600 Hz to 1.5 kHz. The whole tank is less than 1 dB louder on gravel than on grass: the ground changes the tracks' colour, not how loud the tank is. On gravel the tank is as strong from 3 to 5 kHz as it is at 1 kHz, where on dirt it has fallen 7 to 9 dB by then: if anything here proves too much in the headset, it will be that.

The tank's footing is judged again only when it has moved 25 cm or turned 0.03 rad (finding the gravel means looking along the whole course): at most 40 times a second at top speed, each a few thousand sums, with nothing allocated.

## How both players share it

Nothing new goes over the network. Each page plays the tank as it draws it: the engine and tracks from the tank state (the host's simulation, or a guest's copy of it), what each track is on from where that state puts the tank (both pages draw the same roads and course, so both hear the same ground), the throttle from the driver's controls as drawn, the traverse and the handwheels from the turret as drawn (how fast its crank and elevation are changing), the breech and ram from the loading as drawn (the gunner's own, or their packets), and the gun from every shot through `shotListeners`. The short sounds come from changes in that state (`SoundCues`): a new gear engaged, the bump count going up, the lever crossing just short of closed, the rammed flag. The breech opening by itself after a shot is part of the shot's sound, so the cue skips an opening within 1.2 s of a shot (the crewmate's copy of the lever arrives late). Alone, the page hears its own tank.

Each player hears the tank from their own head: the driver has the gearbox at their elbow and the engine well behind, the gunner the breech at their left.

The duck listens to both voices: this player's microphone (an analyser in `src/net/voice.ts`, `micLevel()`) and the crewmate's voice as heard (`remoteLevel()`), passed on as `netLink.micLevel` and `netLink.crewLevel`. Muting either one takes it out of the duck.

## The intercom

`?voice=plain` is the default (plan §2: both players sit within 2 m, and the intercom should sound like headphones). The start-up setting **Crewmate's voice** offers Intercom (the default), From where they stand (the old spatial voice, kept for comparison) and Off. A player who saved From where they stand keeps it. The loopback route for echo cancellation (`?voiceloop=1`) only applies to the spatial voice, so the network test's lagged pair asks for it.

## Settings and Practice

- **Settings → Sound and view → Tank sounds**: On, Quiet (half) or Off, at once and kept (`?sound=1`, `0.5`, `0`). Off suspends the audio context.
- **Practice → Tank sounds → Play each**: the clunk, a second of grind, the ram, the breech, the shot (with its casing), the latch, the bump, a second of the starter, the catch, a cough and a stall, a second apart, without driving or loading.
- **Practice → Tank sounds → Run through**: the continuous sounds without driving or laying the gun, 37 s (`soundTour`): the engine idles, is revved out of gear and let fall; the tank pulls away up a dirt road through the gears to top speed (16.5 s in), then at that speed runs off onto grass (17 s), onto gravel (20.5 s) and back onto the road (24 s), the left track half a second in crossing and the right following 1.2 s behind, so for a moment each time the two tracks are on different ground; it turns left and slows to a halt; the turret traverses under power one way and slowly back; then the traverse crank and the elevation wheel are turned by hand. Only the sounds: the tank and the turret don't move. Pressed again, it stops.
- The line under them says whether sound is on, the engine's beat, what the tracks are on ("tracks on grass", or "dirt (left) and grass (right)"), how long the run through has to go and whether the intercom duck is in.
- Browsers start audio only after a click, a key or Enter VR; until then the line says so. The audio context suspends while the page is hidden or the headset is off.

## Hearing them without a headset

`node scripts/render-sound-preview.mjs [out-dir] [scene ...]` renders the same graph the game plays to WAV files, offline in a headless Chromium (48 kHz stereo; `artifacts/sound-preview/` by default). Six scenes: `tour` (Run through, from the driver's seat), `gunnery` (from the gunner's seat with the engine idling: a shell rammed, the breech closed, the gun fired and its casing, loaded again, the breech opened by hand, the handwheel and the power traverse) and `starting` (from the driver's seat: the starter, the engine catching, a blip of throttle, a gear going in, a grind and the gear after it, a bump, a cough and a stall) `cruise` (from the driver's seat, eight seconds at the revs and speed of the real recording below), `ground` (from the driver's seat at a steady 14 km/h, five seconds on each: grass, the dirt road, gravel, then the left track on the road and the right on the verge, then at a crawl, 4 km/h, on gravel and on grass) and `ground-tracks` (the same with the engine and gears left out: only what the ground changes). Listen on headphones: the clips are placed for a head in the seat, looking forward.

Nobody can listen in the cloud, so the sounds were tuned by measuring these clips: each layer's level, its spectrum band by band and its strongest tones, and spectrograms of the clips. That can show a sound is the right shape (no one tone drowning the rest, levels falling away with frequency as in a real tank, no clicks at a loop's join) but not that it is convincing. That is for the headset check.

## Measured against a real Sherman

John's recording (2026-11-12) is seven and a half seconds of an M4A1 passing at a medium speed, heard from outside. An M4A1 has the Continental radial, not the M4A4's multibank, and the game is heard from inside, so the clip can't be copied; but what it shows of a Sherman's engine and tracks holds for any of them. The clip itself is not in the game or the repository. Measured the same way as the game's own clips:

| What the recording shows | What it meant for the game |
| --- | --- |
| The engine is a stack of tones one cycle apart (15 Hz apart at 1800 rpm, 19 Hz at 2250), strong from about 100 to 400 Hz. The tone at the rate the cylinders fire is not the strongest: the 7th and 13th are, with the 9th (the radial's nine firings) 8 dB under. Only 7% of that energy is where even cylinders would put it | The first sound pass's engine, at the same revs, had 83% of it on the banks' beat and its harmonics: a note at 110 Hz, which is what made the first engine of all sound like a toy. The cylinders are now as different as the recording says they must be (strength, timing, sharpness), which spreads the engine over every tone of the cycle: about 45% is left on the banks' tones, and the strongest tone stands 8 dB over the average (9 dB in the recording) |
| The tones stand 11 dB clear of what lies between them, with the tracks and the wind in that too | The pattern is the engine's own and the same every cycle; little changes from one firing to the next (each firing ±10%, was ±25% with one in sixteen weak) |
| From 500 to 1500 Hz, above its tones, the engine's noise rises and falls by about 15% once a cycle and again twice a cycle | The roar is let through by a breath for every firing, strongest for the strong cylinders, in place of a steady hiss |
| The same bands stand out all through, whatever the revs: near 600, 1050, 1800 and 2450 Hz | The roar has peaks there that don't move with the revs |
| Above 4 kHz the tracks' hiss rises and falls at 26 times a second, a link every 15 cm at 14 km/h, in step with the engine as a gearbox makes it; but only by about 15%. It is never quiet between links | Each track's gate was a row of clicks, shutting between them (the level rose and fell by more than its own average). It now has the lesser strikes of the wheels and the idler in every gap |
| From 100 Hz to 2.5 kHz the level falls gently, about 10 dB in all; above that it carries on to 8 kHz | The game matched it to within about 3 dB a band from 125 Hz to 2.5 kHz once the above was done. Above 3 kHz the game is left 4 to 8 dB under the recording (a hull is between the crew and most of it), where it had been 14 to 18 dB under: the tracks' rings now reach 4.3 kHz and the roar 3.4 kHz |
| Little under 100 Hz (a small microphone, outdoors) | Nothing learned: the rumble through the floor was left, a little quieter |

Comparing the two also showed every placed sound in the game cancelling itself every 160 Hz ("Once, not twice", above), which had nothing to do with the recording but would have coloured everything heard in the headset.

What the recording can't settle: how the A57's five banks differ from a radial's nine cylinders in character (the game keeps the banks' beat, 40 Hz at idle, as its lowest strong tone), and how much of all this reaches a crew inside. Both are for the headset.

## The practice crewmate

Its PC page makes no sound (it would only echo the headset's tank through the PC's speakers), and it has no microphone, so it never ducks the headset's tank. Nothing for it to learn: it hears nothing and says nothing.

## Departures from the building skill

- **No model**: nothing new to draw, so nothing for the z-fighting test.
- **One audio node per short sound played.** A Web Audio buffer source plays once, so each short sound makes one node when it plays. They come a few times a second at most (none in a quiet frame); the continuous sounds' nodes (about 190) are made once and only have their parameters glided, 20 times a second. The listener (the player's head) is moved every frame, with no allocation.
- **Placed by head-related panning, without distance.** Eight panners, one per place; everything is as loud wherever the head is. The cabin is two metres across, so falling off with distance would only make the far seat's sounds thin.

## Headset check

On a Quest 3 after the deploy, with the cache cleared, seated. Wear the headset's own speakers (or headphones) at a comfortable volume. Report what sounds wrong, too quiet or too loud, not only what works: every sound here was tuned by measurement, never by ear.

1. Enter VR alone. Game menu (Y) → **Practice → Tank sounds → Run through**, and listen for 37 s without touching anything. The engine idles as a deep burble (not a diesel's knock, not a buzz), revs up into a rough drone (not a note you could hum) and falls back; then the tank "drives": the engine climbs through four gear changes, the tracks rumble and clatter faster and faster with a gear whine under them; at top speed the ground changes under it three times, the left track a moment before the right: onto grass (the tracks go duller and softer), onto gravel (brighter, with a crunch) and back to the dirt road (the line under the buttons says which); then the left track slows in a turn and you may hear a squeal; then the turret's motor and gears run up, stop and run slowly back; then two bursts of fine ticking from the handwheels. Say which part is least convincing, and whether anything is shrill, buzzy, hollow or tiring.
2. **Play each**. A second apart: the gear lever's click and thud, a second of grinding, a shell going home, the breech slamming, the gun (the blast, the hull ringing, the breech flying open, the echoes off the range, then the casing clanging and bouncing in the bin), the breech opened by hand, a bump, a second of the starter, the engine catching, a cough and a stall. Each should sound as if it happened inside the tank with you. Say which is least convincing.
3. **Practice → Drive the tank**. Turn your head: the engine stays behind you, the gears ahead and to your right, a track on each side. Drive off and change up: the engine's note falls back at each change, and with your foot off at speed it quietens to a hum while the tracks carry on. Turn: the inner track clatters slower. Is the engine too loud or too quiet against the tracks at top speed?
4. Still in **Drive the tank**, which starts on the dirt road up the range: drive up the road, then steer gently right until only the right track is on the grass. The right side should go duller while the left stays as it was. Carry on until both are on grass, then come back onto the road. Then **Practice → Biathlon → Course**, which puts the tank on the course's start, and drive on the gravel: brighter, with a crunch of stones under the tracks that you can pick out grain by grain at a crawl; put one track on the grass inside a bend and that side goes soft. Say whether you can hear the three apart, whether any of them is too much (a hiss, a sizzle) or too little, and whether the change at an edge sounds like driving onto something or like a switch.
5. Shift with a tiller pushed: the grind rasps until you let the tiller go, then the gear clunks in, both from the gearbox at your right. Drive gently into a rock or a wall: a thud and a rattle. Stop the engine and start it: the starter's whine, the catch, the burble running up.
6. **Practice → Load and fire**. Ram a shell, close the breech, fire. The breech sounds come from the gun at your left, wherever the turret points; the blast comes from everywhere, the engine drops away for a moment after it, and the casing lands in the bin by your left leg half a second later. Is the gun loud enough against everything else? Turn the turret with the power grip (motor and gears, rising with the speed), then with the crank, and lay with the elevation wheel (fine ticks).
7. **Settings → Sound and view → Tank sounds**: Quiet halves it, Off silences it at once, On brings it back. Leave it on.
8. With a second headset (or the practice crewmate on a PC, which stays silent): talk. The crewmate's voice sounds plain, like headphones. While either of you speaks, the tank clearly dips and comes back after you stop; say whether you can hear each other over the engine flat out.
9. Press **X** for the perf HUD while driving and firing: report the frame time. The sounds run off the main thread, but there are far more of them than before; the game's JavaScript should be unchanged, and the sound should never crackle or stutter. If it does, say when.
